\outl0\strokewidth0 \strokec2 The Capitalization of Higher Education Subsidies into Local Housing Markets: A Spatial Difference-in-Discontinuities Approach\
\pard\pardeftab720\sa160\partightenfactor0

\fs36 \cf2 1. Introduction and Theoretical Framework\
\pard\pardeftab720\sa320\partightenfactor0

\f1\b0\fs24 \cf2 The spatial distribution of public goods and localized fiscal policies heavily influences household residential choices, fundamentally shaping the trajectory of regional housing markets across the United States. Historically, the theoretical foundation for this phenomenon is rooted in the Tiebout model of residential sorting, which posits that households "vote with their feet" by selecting jurisdictions that offer an optimal bundle of taxes and public amenities. Under the spatial equilibrium framework, any localized advantage\'97such as a superior school district, a lower property tax burden, or enhanced environmental amenities\'97is theoretically capitalized into local land rents and housing prices. While the capitalization of highly localized public goods, most notably K-12 primary and secondary school quality, into housing prices has been extensively documented and rigorously quantified in the urban economics literature, the capitalization of broad, state-level higher education subsidies remains an under-explored frontier in applied microeconomics.\
State-funded public university systems in the United States operate under a residency-based dual-pricing model. These institutions offer heavily discounted in-state tuition rates to verified residents while charging out-of-state students substantial premiums that can be three to five times higher than the resident rate. Over the course of a standard four-year undergraduate degree program, this residency-based "tuition cliff" can generate aggregate household savings exceeding $100,000 for an in-state student relative to a nonresident peer. Furthermore, states host university systems of vastly different academic caliber, research output, and post-graduation salary trajectories. Residents of states with elite, nationally renowned public university systems\'97such as California, Michigan, Virginia, North Carolina, and Texas\'97possess a highly valuable economic asset known as "option value". This represents the inherent right, though not the strict obligation, to access prestigious tertiary education at a heavily subsidized rate.\
Because housing functions as a forward-looking financial asset, these location-specific educational benefits and future cost savings should theoretically be capitalized into local housing wealth. The central hypothesis driving this inquiry is that households will optimize their utility by migrating across state borders to capture either the immediate cost savings of cheaper in-state tuition or the long-term option value of a prestigious public flagship institution. This inter-state migration consequently shifts demand curves in border housing markets, driving a localized price premium on the preferred side of the state boundary. However, estimating the true causal effect of state-level higher education policies presents a profound and complex econometric identification challenge. State borders do not merely represent administrative lines defining tuition eligibility; they are strict geographic boundaries where bundles of disparate institutional, fiscal, and economic policies converge and abruptly shift.\
To rigorously isolate the causal capitalization of higher education subsidies from the noise of simultaneous policy shifts, this report details a comprehensive econometric identification strategy: the Geographic Difference-in-Discontinuities (DiDC) design. By synthesizing the cross-sectional spatial identification power of a Regression Discontinuity (RD) design with the temporal dynamics and trend-differencing of a Difference-in-Differences (DID) framework, the DiDC approach effectively isolates the marginal effect of time-varying changes in state university tuition and institutional caliber from time-invariant border confounders.\
\pard\pardeftab720\sa160\partightenfactor0

\f0\b\fs36 \cf2 2. The Identification Challenge: Compound Treatments at State Borders\
\pard\pardeftab720\sa320\partightenfactor0

\f1\b0\fs24 \cf2 The primary empirical obstacle in measuring the capitalization of state-level policies into localized housing prices is the pervasive presence of "compound treatments" at administrative boundaries. A standard spatial Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) relies fundamentally on the continuity assumption: that all observable and unobservable baseline characteristics vary smoothly and continuously across the geographic threshold. Under this strict assumption, any sudden, discontinuous shift in the outcome variable (housing prices) precisely at the boundary can be attributed solely to the treatment indicator.\
However, state borders represent a severe violation of the standard spatial RDD continuity assumption. State lines are not isolated treatments; rather, they are sharp demarcations for massive bundles of institutional, fiscal, statutory, and economic policies. When a household relocates across a state border, they are instantaneously exposed to a discontinuous shift in state income tax brackets, corporate tax structures, property tax assessment regimes, minimum wage laws, labor market regulations, occupational licensing requirements, and social safety net expansions. In the context of a traditional cross-sectional spatial RDD, the estimated price discontinuity captures the joint, amalgamated effect of all these changing policies simultaneously. It becomes mathematically impossible to isolate the independent, causal capitalization effect of the higher education "tuition cliff" from the broader "tax cliff" or "welfare cliff".\
Compounding this issue is the reality of endogenous residential sorting. Unlike randomized clinical trials, economic agents possess precise, deliberate control over their residential location choices. Households engage in non-random sorting around the geographic cutoff, deliberately choosing to locate on the side of the border that maximizes their specific utility function based on unobservable preferences for tax rates, public services, or political environments. This endogenous sorting directly violates the local randomization assumption that is foundational to the validity of traditional Regression Discontinuity Designs.\
To overcome the intractable problems of compound treatments and endogenous spatial sorting, recent theoretical advancements in spatial econometrics demand the integration of panel data to evaluate policy changes dynamically over time. By observing boundary units across multiple periods before and after a divergence in state tuition policies\'97for instance, observing a market where one state implements a massive tuition hike while the neighboring state strictly freezes its rates\'97researchers can effectively difference out the time-invariant bundle of confounding border policies. This longitudinal differencing forms the theoretical bedrock of the Geographic Difference-in-Discontinuities (DiDC) design, transforming an invalid cross-sectional framework into a robust causal identification strategy.\
\pard\pardeftab720\sa160\partightenfactor0

\f0\b\fs36 \cf2 4. Robustness Checks and Falsification Tests\
\pard\pardeftab720\sa320\partightenfactor0

\f1\b0\fs24 \cf2 Given the complexity of the spatial DiDC framework and the inherently non-random nature of state boundaries, it is absolutely imperative to validate the identification strategy through a series of rigorous robustness checks and falsification tests. These procedures verify that the estimated parameter $\\tau$ truly captures the causal effect of the higher education policy, rather than statistical artifacts or persistent unobserved trends.\
The most critical validation procedure in a spatial RD is the 
\f0\b Placebo Border Test
\f1\b0  (also known as the shifting border test). Utilizing the geospatial manipulation capabilities within the \cf3 \cb4 \strokec3 SpatialRDD\cf2 \cb1 \strokec2  package, researchers can systematically shift the true state border inward by a specified distance (e.g., 50 miles into the control state) and re-estimate the entire DiDC model. Because this "placebo border" does not represent a true administrative discontinuity where tuition policies change, the estimated treatment effect $\\tau$ should be statistically indistinguishable from zero. If a significant discontinuity is detected at the placebo border, it indicates that the model is merely capturing a broader geographic spatial trend rather than a true policy capitalization effect.\
Additionally, researchers must execute 
\f0\b Pre-Trend Event Studies
\f1\b0  to validate the parallel trends assumption foundational to the DID component. By interacting the spatial treatment indicator with a vector of pre-treatment year dummies, researchers can verify that housing prices on either side of the border were evolving dynamically in parallel prior to the divergence in higher education policies. The absence of significant pre-trend coefficients provides vital empirical support that the baseline spatial discontinuity was stable, allowing for a causal interpretation of the post-treatment divergence.\

\f0\b\fs36 \cf2 5. Conclusion\
\pard\pardeftab720\sa320\partightenfactor0

\f1\b0\fs24 \cf2 Evaluating the spatial capitalization of higher education subsidies into local real estate markets transcends the capabilities of traditional hedonic pricing models. Because public university tuition rates and institutional caliber metrics shift strictly and discontinuously along state administrative lines, they present a unique, massive spatial shock to household option value and long-term financial utility. However, state borders are fraught with complex, overlapping, and endogenous fiscal policies that severely confound cross-sectional identification.\
The Geographic Difference-in-Discontinuities (DiDC) model comprehensively detailed in this report offers a highly robust, mathematically rigorous econometric solution to this profound identification problem. By meticulously integrating border-segment-by-year fixed effects, the econometric specification entirely absorbs localized, unobserved geographical and macroeconomic shifts that plague spatial analyses. By leveraging the longitudinal panel structure of the data to dynamically difference out the baseline spatial discontinuity, the model effectively neutralizes the time-invariant "tax cliff" and the insidious biases introduced by historical residential sorting. Finally, by outfitting the model with highly granular programmatic data, the empirical researcher can conclusively isolate the pure, marginal capitalization effect of the "tuition cliff."\
Ultimately, executing this advanced identification design at a national scale using ZCTA-level data and MSE-optimal bandwidth optimization provides a rigorous, defensible pathway to determine whether the immense financial and prestige advantages of state-funded higher education are quietly being captured and absorbed by incumbent landowners in the form of elevated housing premiums. Successful implementation of this DiDC framework promises to deliver vital, highly actionable insights into the hidden spatial inequalities generated by decentralized higher education funding mechanisms in the United States.\
